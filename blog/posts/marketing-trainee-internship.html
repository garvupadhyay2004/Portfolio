What 21 lakh prepaid users taught me about A/B testing

Marketing Trainee · 6 min read

My first real lesson in data-driven marketing

When I joined Bharti Airtel as a Marketing Trainee, I expected to learn about marketing. I didn't expect my biggest lessons to come from SQL queries, customer segments and campaign numbers.

My work involved analysing OTT non-utilization behaviour among 21 lakh+ prepaid users using SQL and Excel, looking for patterns that could explain where customer engagement was dropping.

That experience changed how I looked at marketing. Before that, I thought of marketing campaigns mostly in terms of offers, communication and creativity. Working with telecom data made me realise there is another side: who are we talking to? Why are we talking to them? What happens if we change the offer? And most importantly — did the change actually work?

Starting with 21 lakh customers

The first challenge was scale. Looking at millions of customers is very different from looking at a small classroom dataset — you can't simply open a spreadsheet and start inspecting every row.

I had to approach the data from the perspective of patterns. The objective was to understand OTT non-utilization among a large prepaid customer base and identify behavioural trends that could potentially inform customer engagement strategies.

SQL became one of my primary tools for exploring the data. Instead of looking at individual customers, I started thinking in terms of groups: behaviour, usage patterns, engagement, segments, campaign eligibility.

That shift from individual records to customer segments was one of my first real lessons in analytics.

The question wasn't just "what happened?"

One of the biggest differences between academic analytics and business analytics is the question you're trying to answer.

In a classroom, you might be asked: "What does this dataset show?" In a business environment, the question is more likely to be: "What should we do about it?"

That changes everything. If a segment is showing lower engagement, the analysis doesn't end with identifying the segment. The next question becomes: can we design an intervention? And then: how do we know whether the intervention worked? That's where campaign experimentation came in.

TG vs CG

I worked with Target Group (TG) and Control Group (CG) campaign structures. The idea is straightforward: the target group receives the campaign treatment, and the control group provides a comparison point.

Without a control group, it is difficult to know whether a change in behaviour was actually associated with the campaign or simply happened naturally.

This introduced me to an important principle: a campaign isn't successful just because customers responded to it. The real question is whether the response was meaningfully different from the comparison group. That is the basic thinking behind A/B testing.

The ₹49 experiment

One of the campaigns I worked on involved a ₹49 top-up. The interesting part wasn't simply the offer itself — it was what happened when we compared the campaign outcomes.

The campaign produced a 1.01% positive delta in recharge conversion.

That number might look small at first. But working with a large customer base changes how you think about percentages — a percentage-point movement across a large population can represent a meaningful business impact.

It also taught me something else: a metric is only useful when you understand the population behind it. A 1% change on a few hundred users and a 1% change across millions of customers are very different business situations.

The importance of the control group

The control group became one of the most important concepts for me. Imagine a campaign produces a 5% conversion rate — is that good? You can't answer that without context.

If the control group converted at 4.8%, the campaign might have had a small effect. If the control group converted at 2%, the same 5% campaign result tells a very different story.

That's why TG and CG analysis became more than just two numbers in a report. They provided the basis for asking: what changed because of the campaign? That was my first practical exposure to how experimentation supports marketing decisions.

SQL was only the beginning

One of the things I enjoyed most about the experience was seeing how technical analysis connected with business decisions. SQL helped me retrieve and aggregate the data; Excel helped with analysis and interpretation. But neither tool alone answered the final business question — that step was always interpretation.

  1. Data: a particular customer segment has lower engagement.
  2. Analysis: the segment shows a distinct usage pattern.
  3. Campaign: a targeted offer is introduced.
  4. Experiment: TG performance is compared with CG.
  5. Business question: did the intervention improve the desired behaviour?

That is the point where I started seeing data analytics as more than simply writing queries.

What 21 lakh users taught me

The biggest lesson from this internship wasn't a specific SQL function or Excel formula. It was that scale changes the meaning of data — when you're working with 21 lakh+ customers, a tiny percentage can matter.

It also taught me that customer behaviour isn't something you should look at only after a campaign. It should influence the campaign design itself. The sequence becomes: understand behaviour → identify segment → design intervention → test → measure → learn. And then you repeat the process.

I entered the internship thinking about marketing largely as communication. I left with a much stronger appreciation for marketing analytics — where customer data, experimentation and business decisions come together.

From marketing analytics to AI

Interestingly, this experience also influenced the work I did during my later Airtel internship. The questions became more complex. Instead of only asking, "Which segment should we target?", I started exploring: "Can an AI system understand the business question, find the relevant data, analyse the segment and help generate a recommendation?"

That eventually led me toward Agentic AI, local LLMs, customer analytics and geospatial intelligence during my later internship. The broader project explicitly connected customer segmentation, targeting and campaign analysis with AI-assisted analytics.

Looking back, the two experiences fit together quite naturally. The first taught me how data can influence marketing decisions. The second taught me how AI can become part of the analytical workflow used to make those decisions. And that connection is probably the most interesting part of my Airtel journey.
