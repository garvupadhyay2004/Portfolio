Exploring Youth Segmentation Through Telecom Data

6 min read

Introduction

Understanding customer behavior becomes much more interesting when customer data is connected with where people actually live, study, and move.

During my internship at Bharti Airtel, I worked on several customer analytics problems involving segmentation, campaign analysis, and geospatial intelligence. Towards the later part of the internship, I started exploring a more specific question with my seniors:

Could telecom data help us identify clusters of young users around major educational institutions in Bengaluru?

What started as a segmentation problem gradually became a research-oriented exercise involving large-scale data processing, university-level student estimates, geospatial calculations, and customer-location analysis.

The work was still evolving when my internship ended, so this post is about the approach, observations, and learnings, rather than presenting it as a completed production system.

Starting with Bengaluru's Customer Base

The initial dataset represented approximately 97 lakh Airtel users in Bengaluru.

Working with a dataset of this scale immediately changes the way you think about analytics. A simple filtering operation can become computationally expensive, and connecting users with external geographical information requires careful design.

The first objective was to narrow the population down to a meaningful youth/student segment.

We focused on users broadly associated with the 19–24 age group, with the intention of understanding the potential concentration of young customers around major educational institutions.

Why Focus on Students?

Students are an interesting customer segment because their behavior can be influenced by several factors:

  • Location
  • Campus presence
  • Hostel or accommodation patterns
  • Internet usage
  • Mobility
  • Peer groups
  • Digital lifestyle

Rather than assuming that every young customer behaves in the same way, the idea was to investigate whether geographical concentration could provide an additional dimension for segmentation.

This led to the next question:

Where are these potential student populations concentrated across Bengaluru?

Building the University Dataset

To introduce an educational dimension into the analysis, I created a dataset covering 14 major universities and educational institutions in Bengaluru.

Some of the institutions included:

  • Bangalore University
  • Christ University
  • JAIN University
  • REVA University
  • Kristu Jayanti University
  • CMR University
  • PES University
  • Dayananda Sagar University
  • R.V. College of Engineering
  • Ramaiah Institute of Technology
  • BMS College of Engineering
  • New Horizon College of Engineering
  • IISc
  • IIIT Bangalore

The estimated student population across these institutions was approximately 3.71 lakh.

However, university enrollment numbers and the number of students physically present on campus are not necessarily the same.

Based on the estimates used in the analysis, the physical campus population could be roughly 1.1–1.55 lakh, with approximately 15,000–25,000 students potentially staying on campus or in hostels.

This distinction became important when interpreting the geographical results.

The Scale of the Geospatial Problem

The next challenge was connecting approximately 97 lakh users with the 14 educational institutions.

A direct user-to-university comparison produced roughly:

9.7 million users × 14 institutions ≈ 135.8 million user–college combinations

That is around 13.58 crore candidate combinations.

At this scale, the problem was no longer simply about writing a query.

It became a problem of efficient data processing and spatial computation.

Bringing Geography Into Customer Analytics

Each institution was represented using its geographical coordinates and an estimated campus area.

For each user, we had a corresponding location that could be compared against these university coordinates.

The key calculation was the Haversine distance, which measures the approximate great-circle distance between two latitude/longitude points.

Conceptually:

User Location → University Location → Distance → Spatial Classification

Instead of treating every user as simply "near" or "far" from a university, distance could be used to create more meaningful groups.

For example:

  • Within campus
  • Less than 100 m
  • Less than 500 m
  • 1–2 km
  • 2–5 km
  • 5–7 km
  • 7–10 km

This created a more detailed geographical picture of the potential student population.

Identifying the Youth Segment

After combining the demographic filtering with the geographical analysis, the research identified approximately 1.32 lakh users as a potential youth/student-oriented segment within the scope of the analysis.

The important point here is that this number represents an analytical identification, not a confirmed count of students.

Telecom location data can indicate proximity and patterns, but proximity to a university does not automatically mean that someone is a student there.

That distinction is important when moving from data analysis to business interpretation.

From Segmentation to Location Intelligence

This project changed the way I looked at customer segmentation.

Traditional segmentation might look at variables such as:

Age → Usage → Plan → Customer Segment

The approach I was exploring added another dimension:

Age → Usage → Location → Educational Cluster → Potential Segment

Geospatial information can therefore become an additional layer of customer intelligence.

For example, instead of simply asking:

"Who are our young customers?"

we can begin asking:

"Where are young customers concentrated?"

and eventually:

"What geographical environments are associated with different customer segments?"

Those questions can lead to very different analytical possibilities.

The Technical Side

The analysis involved large-scale data processing and spatial calculations.

The workflow broadly looked like:

Customer Data
↓
Youth/age filtering
↓
University reference dataset
↓
User × University combinations
↓
Haversine distance calculation
↓
Distance-based buckets
↓
Segment identification
↓
Business interpretation

Working with millions of records also reinforced the importance of efficient processing rather than relying on purely row-by-row operations.

This was one of the areas where tools such as SQL, Python, and Spark-based processing became particularly useful.

What We Were Trying to Understand

The larger research question was not simply about finding a number.

We were trying to understand whether telecom customer data could be combined with educational and geographical information to identify meaningful population clusters.

That opens up several potential analytical directions:

  • Student-focused customer segmentation
  • Campus-level customer density
  • Geographic customer concentration
  • Youth mobility patterns
  • Location-based campaign research
  • Understanding differences between campus and surrounding areas

At this stage, these were research directions rather than completed business outcomes.

Why This Remained Research Work

My internship ended while this work was still being explored with my seniors.

Because of that, I don't consider this project a finished production implementation.

There was still more work that could have been done around validation, improving the segmentation methodology, testing assumptions, and determining how the identified population could be interpreted from a business perspective.

For me, that unfinished state is actually an important part of the project.

It showed me that real-world analytics does not always end with a final dashboard or a single model score.

Sometimes the most valuable outcome is discovering a better question to investigate.

What I Learned

This project helped me understand several things beyond the technical implementation.

1. Scale changes analytical thinking.
A calculation that looks simple on a small dataset can become a significant engineering problem when applied to millions of users.

2. Geography adds context.
Customer segmentation becomes more informative when we understand where customers are concentrated.

3. Data does not automatically equal ground truth.
Being near a university does not prove that a user is a student. Analytical assumptions need validation.

4. Research requires knowing the limitations.
An identified segment is not necessarily a business outcome. It needs further validation before being used operationally.

5. Good analytics connects technical work with a question.
The objective wasn't just to calculate distances. It was to understand whether those distances could help answer a meaningful customer-behavior question.

Final Thoughts

This was one of the projects where my internship experience moved beyond conventional customer analytics into geospatial intelligence and research-oriented segmentation.

Starting with approximately 97 lakh Bengaluru users, connecting them with 14 educational institutions, processing roughly 13.58 crore potential user–college combinations, and eventually identifying an approximately 1.32 lakh potential youth/student segment gave me a practical understanding of how demographic and geographical signals can be combined.

The project was unfinished when my internship ended, but the questions it raised were perhaps more valuable than a finished result.

It made me interested in a broader idea:

Customer analytics isn't only about understanding who a customer is. It can also be about understanding where they are, what surrounds them, and how geography changes the way we interpret their behavior.

That is the direction in which I would like to continue exploring customer behavior analytics, geospatial intelligence, and data-driven segmentation.
